Training · August 21, 2026 · 10 min read · By Delia Okonkwo, Coach and Wear Test Lead

Building a winter block that survives February

Almost nobody quits a winter block in December. They quit in the second week of February, and usually for reasons that have nothing to do with fitness.

I have coached winter blocks for eleven years, and the failure pattern is remarkably consistent. The first three weeks go well because the plan is new. Weeks four to eight go well because the routine has formed. Then somewhere around week nine, usually early February, a combination of accumulated fatigue, weather, and darkness turns a fifteen-minute obstacle into a reason to skip. Two skips become a week. A week becomes the end of the block.

What is interesting is that the athletes who make it through are rarely the most motivated. They are the ones who removed the most friction in advance. This article is about that: how to structure the twelve weeks, and more importantly, what to set up so the structure survives contact with a wet Tuesday.

Structure: three blocks of four weeks

The shape we use is three four-week blocks, each with three weeks of building and one week at reduced volume. Twelve weeks in total, which is roughly the span between the start of December and the end of February in the northern hemisphere.

Weeks one to four: rebuild the base. Volume rises gradually, intensity stays deliberately low. Most sessions should be conversational. The purpose here is to accumulate consistent easy work and let tendons and connective tissue adapt, which happens far more slowly than cardiovascular fitness does. The most common error in this phase is running the easy days too hard, which quietly compromises everything after it.

Weeks five to eight: introduce structure. One quality session a week, usually a tempo effort or long intervals at controlled effort. Volume continues to rise slightly. This is where fitness visibly improves and where most people feel best about the block.

Weeks nine to twelve: sharpen and hold. Two quality sessions a week for most athletes, volume flat or slightly reduced. This is the hardest phase psychologically because the novelty is gone and the weather is usually at its worst. It is also where the fitness gains from the first eight weeks get consolidated.

The recovery week at the end of each block is not optional and it is not a reward. It is where adaptation happens. Cut volume by roughly 30% and keep intensity but reduce the amount of it. Athletes who skip recovery weeks do not get fitter faster, they get injured on a slight delay.

The athletes who finish blocks are rarely the most motivated. They are the ones who removed the most friction in advance.

The friction audit

Before week one, sit down and list every step between waking up and starting the session. Not the training, the logistics. A typical list runs to eleven or twelve items: find the kit, find the other sock, check the weather, decide what to wear, fill a bottle, find the headlamp, find the reflective layer, get dressed, get out the door.

Each of those is a small decision, and each one is a chance to not go. Motivation at 6 a.m. in February is a finite and unreliable resource. The goal is to make the session require as few decisions as possible.

What works, in rough order of effect:

  • Lay the kit out the night before, including the socks. This sounds trivial. In our own wear test group, it was the single most-cited habit among athletes who completed the block.
  • Decide the session the night before, and write it down. Deciding what to run while standing in the cold is how easy runs become skipped runs.
  • Own two of the things you wear most. If you train four or more times a week, one pair of shorts and one base layer means your laundry schedule dictates your training schedule. Two of each removes that constraint entirely.
  • Keep a dry layer in the car or by the door. Getting cold after the session is what makes the next session feel unappealing.
  • Fix the visibility problem once. A headlamp with charged batteries and a reflective outer layer, stored in the same place every time.

Kit decisions that matter in winter

I am a coach, not a salesperson, so I want to be careful here: none of this makes you faster. It removes reasons to stay inside, which is a different and more useful thing.

Visibility is the one non-negotiable. If any part of your block happens before sunrise or after sunset, you need to be seen from the front and the back. Reflective detail on a jacket alone is not enough, because a driver's headlights hit your legs first. Reflective on the moving parts, meaning the ankles and wrists, is far more visible than reflective on the torso, because motion is what catches the eye. Our Nightsplit Reflective Shell weaves the reflective element through the fabric rather than printing it, which matters after fifty washes.

Hands and head first, torso second. Most people add a torso layer when they get cold, when the actual problem is extremities. Gloves and a thin beanie do more for comfort in the 30 to 45 degree range than another mid layer, and they do not cause the overheating that ends up making you wetter.

Wind protection over insulation on the legs. Legs generate a lot of heat and rarely need insulating. What they need is a windproof front. That is the entire argument for a wind-panel half tight over a heavier full tight for most winter sessions.

Something that dries between sessions. If you train on consecutive mornings, anything that is still damp at 6 a.m. will not get worn. This is the practical case for grid fleece over heavy cotton, and it is why our Thermal Grid Crew is the piece most of our wear testers ask for a second of.

What to wear at each temperature is covered in more detail in our layering article, including the rule about being slightly cold in the doorway.

The three places blocks actually break

The illness week. Somebody in your house gets sick and you lose five days. The mistake is trying to make up the missed volume. Do not. Return at roughly 70% of where you left off for a few days, then resume the plan where it would have been. A block with one missed week and a sensible return is worth far more than a block with one missed week and a panicked catch-up.

The single hard session that goes badly. One session at the wrong pace on tired legs makes people doubt the whole plan. It is one data point in a hundred. Judge a block on the trend across four weeks, not on a Tuesday.

The genuinely dangerous weather day. Ice is the one condition where the right answer is to move indoors or move the session. Running on ice is not toughness, it is a six-week injury waiting to happen. Have a default indoor alternative decided in advance so that decision takes ten seconds.

A note on what the block is for

The point of a winter block is not the twelve weeks. It is the position it puts you in during March, when the weather improves and you can start doing specific work from a base that took three months to build. Athletes who train opportunistically through winter and start their real preparation in spring spend six weeks getting back to where the consistent athlete started.

That is worth remembering in week nine, when it is dark and raining and the session is a set of intervals you do not want to do. You are not training for that session. You are training so that March is available to you.

Lay the kit out tonight.
