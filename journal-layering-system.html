Training · June 18, 2026 · 9 min read · By Rosalind Achebe, Product Development Lead

The three-layer system, rebuilt for people who sweat

Standard layering advice was written for hikers. Training breaks almost every assumption it makes.

If you have read anything about layering, you have read the same three sentences. A base layer to move moisture, a mid layer to trap heat, a shell to block wind and rain. It is a good model. It is also built on an assumption that does not hold for anyone doing intervals: that your heat output is roughly constant.

A hiker walking uphill with a pack produces a fairly steady amount of heat for hours. An athlete running a session might warm up at 40% of maximum effort, hit 90% for four minutes, drop back to 50%, repeat that five times, then stand still for ten minutes at the end while their heart rate falls. Heat output across that session varies by roughly a factor of five, and moisture production varies with it. The layering system that is correct at minute six is wrong at minute twenty and dangerous at minute fifty-five.

So the useful question is not what to wear. It is what to wear at which point, and which piece you are prepared to carry when it comes off.

Start with the rule that actually helps

The most useful rule we know is the twenty-degree rule, and it is deliberately crude. Dress for a temperature about twenty degrees Fahrenheit warmer than the thermometer reads. If it is 40 degrees outside, dress as though it were 60. You should feel slightly cold standing at the door. If you feel comfortable in the doorway, you are overdressed for minute ten.

This works because your body produces most of its warming within the first eight to ten minutes of moderate effort. Almost everyone who is uncomfortable during a session made the decision that caused it before they left the house.

The rule needs three adjustments. Wind is worth roughly ten degrees at any speed above a light breeze, more if you are wet. Rain is worth about fifteen degrees, because water conducts heat away from you roughly twenty-five times faster than still air. And low-output sessions, meaning easy runs, recovery work or anything with long standing rests, need less adjustment than hard sessions. If you are doing intervals with two-minute standing recoveries, you are effectively running two different sessions and need to dress for both.

You should be slightly cold in the doorway. Comfortable in the doorway means overdressed by minute ten.

The base layer's real job

The base layer is not there to keep you warm. It is there to get sweat off your skin and into the next layer, where it can evaporate somewhere that is not against your body. A base layer that holds moisture is worse than no base layer at all, because wet fabric against skin during a rest interval is how you get genuinely cold.

This is where cotton earns its bad reputation. Cotton holds up to 27 times its weight in water and gives it up slowly. During a steady effort you may never notice. During a session with rest intervals, or on a cold walk back to the car, a wet cotton shirt will pull heat out of you at a rate that turns an inconvenience into a problem.

The two credible options are fine merino and technical synthetics, and they fail in different ways. Merino holds around 30% of its weight in moisture before it feels wet, which is why it stays comfortable across a wider range of conditions, and it resists odor well enough to wear for multiple sessions. It also dries slowly. A soaked merino base layer stays soaked for a long time.

Synthetics move moisture faster and dry faster, which makes them better for high-output sessions where you will be genuinely wet. They also hold odor, unless the odor control is built into the yarn rather than sprayed on the surface, in which case it survives washing. That distinction is worth checking on any product page, including ours.

The mid layer is the one people get wrong

Most people treat the mid layer as insulation, choose the warmest thing they own, and end up soaked from the inside. A training mid layer has a harder job than a hiking one: it must trap enough air to keep you warm at low output and move enough moisture to not become a swamp at high output.

That is why grid fleece exists. The knit leaves channels of open space against the skin so warm air sits in the pockets while moisture travels along the channels toward the surface. You get insulation that behaves more like a wick. Our Thermal Grid Crew is a 190 gsm version, deliberately on the light side, because most people buy a mid layer that is too warm for the way they actually train.

The test for whether your mid layer is right: after twenty minutes of steady effort, you should be warm but not sweating through it. If the inside face is visibly wet at that point on a cold day, it is too warm, not too thin.

The shell is a wind decision before it is a rain decision

Most days that feel unbearable are wind days, not rain days. A windproof layer that is not waterproof solves eighty percent of bad weather at a fraction of the weight and cost, and it breathes far better. That is the argument for a wind vest or a light anorak as the piece that lives in your bag year-round.

Full waterproofing is a different purchase and involves a genuine trade-off. Every waterproof membrane restricts moisture movement to some degree, no matter what the breathability number says. A 20,000mm shell like our Velocity Shell will keep sustained rain out, and on a mild wet day you will still finish damp from the inside. That is physics, not a defect, and anyone claiming otherwise is selling something.

The practical rule: buy the lightest waterproof that will genuinely keep the rain out for the length of your longest session, then dress lighter underneath it than you think you need.

What to wear, by temperature band

These are starting points for a moderate to hard session, assuming little wind and no rain. Adjust down a layer if it is windy, up a layer for easy running.

  • Above 60 F. Singlet or tee and shorts. Nothing else. A cap if the sun is out.
  • 50 to 60 F. Short sleeve and shorts, with a wind vest carried rather than worn. Most people overdress in this band.
  • 40 to 50 F. Long sleeve base layer and shorts, or a tee and half tights. Add gloves before you add a layer to the torso, because hands lose comfort first.
  • 30 to 40 F. Base layer plus a light mid layer, full length tights, a beanie and gloves. This is where grid fleece earns its price.
  • 20 to 30 F. Base layer, mid layer, wind shell, tights, beanie, gloves. Cover the wind-facing surfaces rather than adding thickness everywhere.
  • Below 20 F. The same, plus a windproof panel over the thighs and a neck cover. Below about 10 F, treat every session as an easy session.

The part nobody plans for

The most common mistake is not what happens during the session. It is what happens in the fifteen minutes afterwards, when you stop producing heat and are wearing wet clothing in cold air. That is when people get properly cold, and it is why every experienced athlete carries a dry layer.

It does not need to be technical. A dry cotton hoodie in the car is fine for that job, because you are not going to sweat in it. What matters is that it is dry and it goes on within a couple of minutes of stopping, before your core temperature starts falling.

If you take one thing from this article, take that one. It costs nothing, it is not a product recommendation, and it will improve more of your winter sessions than any jacket will.
