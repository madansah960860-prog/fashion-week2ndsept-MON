Materials · July 9, 2026 · 10 min read · By Tobias Marchetti, Materials Manager

What recycled nylon actually is, and where it falls short

Recycled content is the most-used claim in performance apparel and one of the least understood. Here is what is behind the number on our labels.

When a product page says 100% recycled nylon, it is making a claim about where the raw polymer came from and nothing else. It says nothing about how the fabric was dyed, how it was finished, how far it travelled, or how long it will last. Those four things usually matter more to the total footprint than the feedstock does, which is why recycled content alone is a weak signal.

We use a lot of recycled polyester and nylon, roughly 71% of our fabric volume by weight. We think it is the right default. We also think our customers should understand exactly what they are and are not buying, so this article is written to be used against us.

Where the material comes from

There are two feedstocks in common use, and they are not equivalent.

Post-consumer material comes from products that reached an end user and were then collected: plastic bottles, discarded fishing nets, carpet. This is the material most people picture when they read the word recycled, and it is genuinely diverting waste from landfill or ocean.

Post-industrial material comes from manufacturing waste: offcuts, spinning waste, off-spec yarn that never left the factory. This is also recycling in a real sense, and it is generally cleaner and more consistent to work with. It is less impressive, though, because a well-run factory has always had an incentive to reuse its own waste. Selling that as an environmental achievement is a stretch.

Both are legitimate. The problem is that a label saying recycled rarely distinguishes them. Our own line uses post-consumer PET for polyester and a mixture of post-industrial and post-consumer nylon waste, certified under the Global Recycled Standard, which requires chain-of-custody documentation from the recycler through to the finished fabric. If you want the certificate numbers for a specific style, we will send them.

Recycled content tells you where the polymer came from. It tells you nothing about dye, finish, freight or lifespan.

Mechanical versus chemical recycling

Once the waste is collected, there are two routes to turn it back into fiber.

Mechanical recycling shreds, melts and re-extrudes the material. It uses less energy and is far more common. The drawback is that polymer chains shorten each time, so fiber strength degrades slightly with every cycle. In practice mechanically recycled polyester is blended with virgin material or spun to a lower specification. It also carries color from the input stream, which limits what shades you can dye it to. This is why so much recycled fabric is black or dark: it is easier to dye down than up.

Chemical recycling breaks the polymer back to its monomers and rebuilds it. The output is essentially indistinguishable from virgin material, can be dyed to any shade, and can theoretically be recycled indefinitely. It is also energy intensive and, at present, expensive. Capacity worldwide is still small relative to demand.

Almost all recycled nylon in outdoor and performance apparel today, including in our shells, is mechanically recycled or produced through depolymerisation of pre-sorted nylon 6 waste. When a brand does not say which route was used, mechanical is the safe assumption.

The part almost nobody mentions: dye

Dyeing and finishing typically account for a large share of a fabric's water use, chemical load and energy consumption. Depending on the fabric and process, wet processing can rival or exceed fiber production in total impact. A fabric can be 100% recycled and still have been dyed in a mill with poor water treatment, which is a worse outcome than virgin fiber dyed responsibly.

This is why we care more about which mill a fabric comes from than about the recycled percentage alone. Eleven of our fabrics come from bluesign approved mills, which means the chemical inputs, worker safety and water discharge have been assessed at the facility level. That certification is harder to obtain and more informative than a recycled-content claim, and it is far less commonly advertised because it does not compress into a marketing line.

Dark colors also matter for a duller reason: they hide the color variation that comes with mechanically recycled feedstock, so mills can run higher recycled percentages. Our black and charcoal pieces genuinely carry more recycled content than our lighter ones, and we would rather explain that than let people assume it is a design preference.

Where recycled synthetics fall short

Three honest problems.

Shedding. Recycled polyester sheds microfibers in the wash exactly like virgin polyester does. Recycling the feedstock does nothing about this. The mitigations that actually work are washing less often, washing full loads on cold and gentle cycles, using a filter bag, or fitting a machine filter. We would rather say that than pretend the recycled label solves it.

The bottle question. Turning a PET bottle into fabric takes it out of the bottle-to-bottle recycling stream, where it could have been recycled repeatedly. Turning it into a jacket is, for now, a one-way trip. It is still better than virgin polyester and landfill, but it is not the closed loop that the imagery implies.

Blends. A fabric that is 92% polyester and 8% elastane cannot currently be recycled fiber-to-fiber at commercial scale, because separating the two is not economic. Nearly every stretch performance fabric on the market has this problem, ours included. The industry has been promising a solution for a decade. When one exists, we will use it and say so.

Three claims we will not make

  • That recycled means low impact. It means lower impact than the virgin equivalent, in the fiber production stage only, all else equal. All else is rarely equal.
  • That a recycled jacket is carbon neutral. We have not completed a full footprint across our supply chain, so any neutrality claim from us would be arithmetic performed on a guess.
  • That buying a new jacket helps the environment. It does not. The lowest-impact option is the jacket you already own. If yours is repairable, repair it, and if it is one of ours we will do that for you at cost.

What we think you should actually check

If you are comparing two technical garments, the recycled percentage is one of the least informative numbers on the page. More useful, roughly in order:

  • Is the water repellent finish PFAS-free? This is a genuine chemical hazard question and the industry has largely moved. Ours went PFAS-free across the range in 2024.
  • Does the brand publish the fabric weight and full composition? Brands that hide gsm are usually hiding something about durability.
  • Is there a repair program, and what does it cost? A repair offer with a real price list is a longevity commitment. A repair offer with no price is marketing.
  • Are there facility-level certifications? bluesign, WRAP, SA8000 and the Responsible Wool Standard say more about how something was made than a fiber claim does.
  • How long has the style been in the line? A garment that has been sold unchanged for four years is a durability signal that no certification captures.

By that last measure our Thermal Grid Crew is the piece we are proudest of. Same fabric, same pattern, same price structure since 2021, restocked rather than reinvented. That is the least glamorous form of sustainability there is, and probably the most effective.

Our full material sourcing, certifications and limitations are laid out on the sustainability page. If you find a claim there that we cannot document, tell us and we will change the page.
